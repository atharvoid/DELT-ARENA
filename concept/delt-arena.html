Concept review, round two25 November 2026

Delt Arena

Three directions, six working screens. Round one looked like every other fitness app, and the "Hurts / Swap / Note" row showed it. This round starts each direction from something lifters already know by heart: a race engineer on the radio, a thermal camera, and the music player they touch between every set.

Every phone below works. Hold the yellow button, press on the body, swipe the cover, drag the faders. The numbers are example data.

Pit Wall

Your coach is a race engineer in your ear. You log a set by saying it into your earbuds, and the coach answers with short calls between sets. The phone can stay on the bench, face up, readable from two metres.

The set
Hold the yellow button (or squeeze an earbud) and say "eighty-two five for six, two left". It's logged and the next call comes back. Weight is set at 158 px so you can read it lying on the bench.
The coach
A radio log of decisions. Each call gets a "Copy" to accept it or a counter-call. Pain gets reported the way you'd tell a person: "my left shoulder pinched at the bottom".
The risk
It leans on voice. Some people won't talk out loud in a gym, so every call also has buttons. Racing imagery can turn into costume if it goes past type and timing.

Thermal

Your body is the dashboard. Every hard set warms the muscles it trains, so your week shows up like a thermal image: overworked muscles burn white, neglected ones stay blue. You see in one glance which part is lacking, before reading a number.

The set
The body is on screen while you lift. Log the set and the muscle you just trained warms up in front of you. Weight and reps sit on picker wheels, already set to today's target.
The coach
Findings are pinned to the body with leader lines, the way a camera labels a hotspot. "Apply" re-plans Thursday, and you can watch the hamstrings warm and the quads cool.
The risk
Heat is a metaphor. People must not read it as an injury or inflammation scan, so the scale is always labelled in sets, never in degrees.

Now Lifting

A session plays like an album. The set you're on is the track on screen, its cover is your loaded barbell seen end-on, and one swipe logs it. The coach's week is a mixing desk: drag a muscle's fader and the program rewrites itself.

The set
Swipe the cover right to log it exactly as planned, or left to swap the exercise. The cover rings are the plates on one sleeve, so you load from the picture.
The coach
An eight-fader mix, one per muscle, with the target range shaded. "Auto-balance" moves the faders for you, and dragging one yourself writes the program change. The coach warns if your edit adds too much.
The risk
It's the most playful of the three. Serious lifters may want more numbers on the first screen, so the chips under the title carry every value and open an editor.

My pick

Thermal, as the spine of the app. It's the only one where you understand your training before reading anything, and that's exactly the "hams underworked, quads overworked" problem you described. No app in the research shows the body this way. Pressing on the body to mark pain is also far more natural than any pain button.

I'd take two things from the others. Now Lifting's swipe-to-log and end-on plate covers would become the live set screen. Pit Wall's voice radio would become an earbud mode you can switch on for heavy days. Tell me if you'd rather go all in on one direction instead.

The system plan behind all three

What the field does today

Strength loggers (Hevy, Strong, Fitbod, Alpha Progression) log well. A set takes three to five touches, and Strong's watch app gets it down to one tap. The complaints are about the basics around the log: rest timers that can't be muted or don't reach the lock screen (Alpha Progression reviews), and little help deciding what to lift next (Hevy review). Fitbod programs for you, but users report repeated exercises and weights that feel "randomized or unsound" (Trustpilot).

Outdoor GPS is solved. Gym machines aren't: Apple Watch treadmill distance often comes out short of the console's, and you can't type in the machine's number (Apple forums). GymKit covers only some machines in some gyms (9to5Mac). The Bluetooth Fitness Machine standard exists and a few apps read it (Bluetooth SIG).

Whoop under-reads heart rate during lifting (Whoop community). Strava's AI summaries get called generic (Cycling Weekly), while Whoop Coach earns credit when it reads the user's own data (T3). The Outsiders: Athlete Tracker, a 2026 Apple Design Award finalist, was recognised for a readiness score built from load, sleep and resting heart rate (Apple).

Rules the coach can stand on

  • About 12 to 20 hard sets per muscle per week for trained lifters, calibrated per person (2022 review, Schoenfeld 2017).
  • Each muscle at least twice a week (2016 meta-analysis).
  • Progression by reps in reserve does at least as well as fixed percentages (Helms 2018).
  • Running load against the four-week average is a heads-up only, because the evidence for a hard cut-off is disputed (Gabbett 2016, Sedeaud 2020).

Reddit and some journal sites were blocked from this session, so app complaints come from reviews and forums. Read them as signals.

How the coach works

A deterministic engine computes every number: next weight, sets per muscle, running load. The language model explains those numbers, answers questions and drafts drills from a reviewed library. Every suggestion can show its working.

Onboarding to program

Goal, height, weight, training age, days per week, equipment and injuries pick a proven template, which is then adjusted. The model fills in a known structure and never invents a split from nothing.

Custom program check

When you build your own program, the same ranges run on it before you train it: weekly sets per muscle, sets per muscle in one session, frequency. You get a warning with the reason, and you're never blocked.

Cardio capture

Outdoor runs, rides and walks start with one tap and pause by themselves. Machines pair over Bluetooth where they support it, and older consoles get read from a photo. Pool swims need a watch.
