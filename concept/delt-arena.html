What the field does today

Gym logging, GPS cardio, recovery and AI coaching live in separate apps today, and each one is weak where the others are strong.

Strength loggers: Hevy, Strong, Fitbod, Alpha Progression

Logging works well. A set usually takes three to five touches: pick the exercise, adjust weight and reps, tick it, and the rest timer starts. Strong's watch app gets it down to one tap on a set ring. Complaints are about the basics around the log: rest timers that can't be muted or don't reach the lock screen (Alpha Progression reviews), plate calculators behind paywalls, and little help deciding what to lift next (Hevy review). Fitbod does program for you, but users describe repeated exercises and suggested weights that feel "randomized or unsound" (review, Trustpilot).

Cardio: Strava, Runna, Apple Workout

Outdoor GPS is solved. Gym machines aren't. Apple Watch treadmill distance regularly comes out well short of the console's, and Workout won't let you type in the machine's number (Apple forums). Apple's GymKit pairs with machines, but only some machines in some gyms (9to5Mac). The Bluetooth Fitness Machine standard exists and a few apps read it (Bluetooth SIG). We found no app that reads the console from a photo. Runna is the most motivating plan app, and also the one users blame for plans that ramp too fast (TechRadar).

Recovery and AI coaches: Whoop, Strava Athlete Intelligence

Whoop's wrist sensor under-reads heart rate during lifting, so an hour of heavy training can score like a short walk (Whoop community). Strava's AI summaries get called generic and patronising because they restate the numbers and ignore longer trends (TrainerRoad forum, Cycling Weekly). Whoop Coach gets credit when it interprets the user's own data and loses it when it gives generic advice (T3). Apple's 2026 Design Award finalists include The Outsiders: Athlete Tracker, recognised for a readiness score built from load, sleep and resting heart rate (Apple). That's the bar for this category.

What the evidence supports for the coach's rules

  • Weekly volume: more hard sets grow more muscle up to a point. A 2022 review puts 12 to 20 weekly sets per muscle as a sound standard for trained lifters (PubMed; earlier dose-response work: Schoenfeld 2017). Ranges vary by person, so they start as defaults and get calibrated per lifter.
  • Frequency: train each muscle at least twice a week (2016 meta-analysis).
  • Progression: loading by reps in reserve does at least as well as fixed percentages (Helms 2018). Double progression is coaching practice rather than a tested protocol.
  • Running load: the acute:chronic ratio (a 0.8 to 1.3 "safe zone") is widely used and also disputed (Gabbett 2016, against: Sedeaud 2020). Delt Arena treats it as a heads-up, never a block.

Upgrades you can feel

Each of these fixes a specific friction in today's apps. None of them needs research-grade technology. Most are rules and good defaults.

The set is already filled in

Every set opens with today's prescription (weight, reps, target reps in reserve) worked out from last session. If you hit it, one tap logs it. You only touch the steppers when the set went differently. A set that matches the plan costs one tap instead of three or four.

Plates, worked out

The prescribed weight is drawn as plates per side in the competition colour code, using the plates your gym actually has. Nobody does bar maths between sets.

Machines report themselves

Many recent treadmills, rowers, bikes and stair climbers broadcast speed, distance, floors and time over the Bluetooth Fitness Machine standard (FTMS). Delt Arena pairs with them when you start the block. For older machines, you photograph the console at the end and the app reads the numbers off it.

Outdoor cardio starts itself

Run, ride and walk start with one tap and pause automatically. A watch handles pool swims (lengths, stroke and pace come from its motion sensors). A phone can't do that, so the app is plain about it.

One weekly ledger for every muscle

Each exercise maps to the muscles it trains, with partial credit for helpers (a bench press counts fully for chest and half for triceps). The week adds up into hard sets per muscle, drawn against an evidence-based target range. Too much quad and too little hamstring shows up as a picture, then as a suggested fix.

A coach that shows its working

A deterministic engine calculates every number: progression, volume, running load. The language model explains those numbers, answers questions and drafts drills, and every card has a "Show the math" view. Users trust a suggestion more when they can check it.

Something hurts, one tap

"Something hurts" sits on every set. You pick the spot, the movement and how bad it is. The coach swaps or modifies the exercise right away, suggests drills with a safety note, and keeps track if the same flag comes back.

Programs get a sanity check

When you build your own program, the app checks weekly sets per muscle, sets per muscle per session, and how often you train each muscle. It warns, with the reason, when something goes well over or under range. It never blocks you.

How the system fits together

1. Set upGoal (fat loss, bulk, keep muscle, hypertrophy, strength, endurance), height, weight, training age, days per week, session length, equipment, injuries.
2. ProgramA template picked by goal and days per week, then adjusted for equipment and injuries. The model can't make up a split; it only fills in a structure that's known to work.
3. TrainPrescribed sets, one-tap logging, machine pairing, GPS. Pain flags and swaps happen inside the set.
4. ReadEvery session updates estimated 1RMs, hard sets per muscle, running load and recovery inputs (sleep and resting heart rate, if a wearable is connected).
5. AdjustNext session's targets, swaps for imbalances, deload weeks. Small changes apply automatically. Bigger ones wait for one tap.

The data model

  • Session, then blocks. A block is strength (exercise, sets) or cardio (modality, source, samples).
  • Set: weight, reps, reps in reserve, tempo (optional), side (for single-arm and single-leg work), flags.
  • Cardio samples: time series of pace, speed, incline, floors or steps, heart rate and GPS, depending on the source.
  • Exercise library: muscle weights per exercise, equipment, movement pattern, joint stress profile (used for pain swaps).
  • All of it maps to HealthKit and Health Connect, so nothing is trapped.

The coach engine

  • Progression: double progression with a reps-in-reserve check. Hit the top of the rep range at the target effort and the weight goes up one plate step. Miss it twice and it holds or drops.
  • Volume: hard sets per muscle per week against a target range for your goal and training age.
  • Running: this week's load against your four-week average, plus a cap on weekly distance jumps. Past about 1.3 you get a heads-up, never a block, because the evidence for a hard line is mixed.
  • One fatigue picture: lifting volume and cardio load sit on the same weekly view, so a heavy leg week and a long run aren't judged in isolation.
  • Language model: turns findings into short cards, answers chat in context, drafts drills from a reviewed library. Medical language always carries the safety note.

The look: a competition platform

Most fitness apps reach for black screens with neon green, or soft wellness pastels. Delt Arena takes its look from the lifting platform: chalk-pale ground, dark ink, and the colour code of competition bumper plates. Lifters know that code without thinking (red 25, blue 20, yellow 15, green 10, white 5), so the colours carry meaning without a legend.

Blue is the only colour you can tap. Red means over range or stop. Yellow means under range or watch. Green means done or in range. Big numbers are set in a condensed stadium face, so a weight reads from a metre away with the phone on the bench. The rest of the interface stays native: SF Pro for text, system sheets and tab bar, glass only on the bars that float over content (the way Apple's Liquid Glass guidance asks). Dark mode is the same platform under arena lights. Try both with your system setting.

What I'd decide next

  • Does this direction match what you had in mind? The platform look, one-tap prescribed sets, and a coach that's a ledger first and a chat second.
  • Platform order: iOS first (HealthKit, Live Activities on the lock screen during rest, Apple Watch for swims), Android second?
  • The exercise-to-muscle table and the drill library need a qualified coach and a physio to review them before launch. They're what makes the coach credible.
  • Next screens to design: onboarding (goal to program in under two minutes), the outdoor run with its live map, and the program builder with its warnings.
